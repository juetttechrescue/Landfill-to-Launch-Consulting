Be the alchemist of your own waste.

You have something to get rid of, and more options than you know about. Revivo shows you all of them, then helps you pick the right one for you. Save money. Make money. Donate it. Give it to a particular organization. Make the strongest environmental impact. Or just not drive anywhere. Or combine them: rank two or three of those and every option gets weighed against all of them, in the order you actually care about. Tailored to you, not to the average person.

How Revivo already knows

Revivo is a database we built, not a search we run. County records, research datasets, social feeds, search engines and phone calls: dozens of separate sources, checked one at a time and merged into a single record for every material.

  • Research done on the ground: calls, visits, and what each place says it will actually take at the gate. About 71 percent of options are checked against the organization's own listing, every card says which of the three tiers it is in, and the three add up to one hundred
  • The seasonal roundups, coat drives and one-person operations that appear in no index anywhere
  • Public records from every waste facility, program and collection day in the county
  • County datasets, census tract research and facility records, pulled together so one search covers all of them
  • Social feeds and neighborhood boards where people give things away and ask for what they need
  • What residents tell us as the pilot runs, about what worked and what turned them away
  • And everything a search engine can reach, already read, checked and sorted, so you never have to

A search engine or an AI reads only what is public, and hands you links. Revivo has all of that too, and then the layers underneath it: research databases you cannot query from a browser, feeds that sit behind logins, and the things that were never online in the first place. All of it gathered into one intelligent database for waste and where it should go. You say what you have and what you want. It gives you the answer.

Why it exists

I'm Natalie. I'm an economics student, and I created Juett Tech Rescue to fill a gap in convenient and affordable, and therefore accessible, electronic waste management.

With Juett Tech Rescue I try to cover everything a resident could possibly want in that one sector. Data wiping, recycling, refurbishment, resale, donation. One service, at your door, and you might get money back instead of paying to make it disappear.

Then I realized people have a lot of options for getting rid of a lot of things, and they mostly do not know what to do with any of it. I could not build a Juett Tech Rescue for every single sector. So I built this instead.

Revivo does not pick for you. It shows what is actually available in Boulder County and sorts it by what you want: the cheapest way out, the most money back, the least driving, the fastest, or the best outcome for the material. The most convenient and affordable option is different for everybody, which is the whole point.

This first month is completely free for Boulder County residents. It's a proof of concept, and I want to know whether it works before I ask anyone for money. If it tells you something wrong, tell me.

Method

Every option is scored on what it costs you, how long it takes, how much work it is, and what happens to the material. The preference you pick decides which of those matter.

Spending the least and making the most are different goals

They sit on the same dollar axis but optimize opposite things. Cheapest counts only money guaranteed to stay in your pocket, so an option that pays off just 40% of the time scores as zero there. Money mode uses expected value, price multiplied by how often it actually sells, and accepts the waiting and the effort in exchange. One slider cannot express both.

Resale numbers show their odds

An option that quietly advertises the price it fetches when it sells, and hides how rarely that happens, is lying. Every resale row here shows both.

Nine preferences, because people want different things

Money is only two of them, and they pull opposite ways. Beyond that: least effort, speed, and material outcome. Then four that most tools ignore entirely. Whether it reaches a person who needs it rather than a shredder. Whether the value stays in Boulder County. Whether you have to deal with a stranger at all, which matters more to more people than anyone building these tools admits. And whether you walk away with a deduction, which is frequently worth more than the resale you were considering.

Recycling is the fourth-best environmental answer

Most tools collapse the whole environmental question into one word. It is at least five separate questions, and they disagree with each other. Does the object survive intact, or does it get shredded into feedstock? How far does it travel and how much energy does the processing take? Can anyone actually tell you where it ends up, or does the trail stop at a private buyer? Does any of it get buried? Repairing something beats donating it, donating beats recycling, and recycling beats burning. There is a separate ranking here for each of those, plus one combined measure.

All of Boulder County, not just the city

Longmont, Lafayette, Louisville, Superior, Erie, Lyons, Nederland, Niwot and Gunbarrel each run their own collection and their own drop-offs, and the mountain communities are an hour from Recycle Row. Every item carries an option pointing you at your own municipality first, because for a lot of residents that is a shorter drive and a lower fee than anything in Boulder.

Safety rules run before ranking

Hazardous materials are filtered out of landfill routes before any scoring happens, so no preference weight can outvote them. Open "what we ruled out" under any hazardous item to see what was removed and why.

Where the data comes from

Five layers, not one. Addresses, hours and fees come from each organization's own published information and are kept current against a places directory. Material-to-facility mapping comes from national recycling directories for anything the county has not catalogd. Resale figures come from live sold-listing comps rather than asking prices. Free and wanted listings come from neighborhood exchange feeds. Pop-ups, church drives and one-person operations come from local search sweeps and from residents submitting them, because they exist in no database anywhere.

The compost rules changed, and most guides have not caught up

Since April 2023 the Front Range compost system takes two categories and nothing else: food scraps, and yard and plant trimmings. Paper towels, napkins, tissues, shredded paper, tea bags and coffee filters are all out. So is every compostable cup, plate, utensil and takeout container, even certified ones, because A1 Organics found that inspectors cannot tell the genuine from the lookalikes once a load arrives. No bags at all, with one exception for a three-gallon certified countertop liner. Put anything else in the cart and your hauler may simply not collect it.

Where your glass actually goes

Boulder County's glass is trucked to Momentum Recycling in Broomfield, Colorado's first bottle-to-bottle plant. It is washed, sorted into green, brown and clear, and sent to O-I Glass, which supplies bottles to Molson Coors, Anheuser-Busch and New Belgium. A bottle can be back on a shelf in about thirty days. Colorado still only recycles somewhere between a quarter and a third of its glass, which is why the jar you keep and reuse counts for more than the jar you put in the bin.

Why reuse beats recycling, in numbers

Resource Central alone has diverted more than 74 million pounds of building materials from landfill, and in 2025 moved over five million pounds while saving local residents around three million dollars. Their reclaimed stock sells for 50 to 75% below retail. That is what the "keep it whole and in use" ranking is pointing at: the same door, still a door, in somebody else's house, instead of being ground into feedstock.

Every card says whether it was checked

Three tiers, and they add up to one hundred. Seventy-one percent of the options carry a link to the organization's own published page, and the address, the hours, the fee and what they take at the gate were read off it. Those say so on the card. Twenty-four percent need no source at all, because "keep it and use it yourself" or "post it to your neighbors" is not a claim about a business. The last five percent are real places with no public page to link: a church drive, a lending shelf, a one-person operation. They say exactly that, rather than borrowing the credibility of a citation they do not have. A tool that quietly presents a guess as a fact is worse than one that admits the gap.

How it stays current

Every option that links out to an organization was checked against that organization's own page by hand, and the link sits on the card so you can check it yourself. Nothing here claims to re-verify itself overnight: a static page cannot, and saying otherwise would be the same kind of quiet overstatement this tool exists to avoid. Options with no link are local knowledge, submitted by residents or gathered from pop-ups and drives that have no website at all. Those are the ones nothing else has, and they are also the ones most likely to change, so they are marked differently on purpose.

What none of it covers

No national directory knows that CHaRM charges three dollars per vehicle and waives it if you arrive by bike, or that the county hazardous facility is free but wants proof of address. Those local specifics are maintained by hand, and they are the reason this is more useful than a search box.
